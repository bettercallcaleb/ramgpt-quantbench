The first sound I heard was ice ticking against the aluminum hull, a dry, impatient rattle that made the boat seem smaller than it was. We had left the harbor at 05:40, before sunrise, with the lighthouse supply manifest clipped under the clear cover on my knee. The winter run was supposed to be routine: diesel filters, two propane cylinders, a crate of lamp housings, sealed food bins, mail, and a replacement weather sensor for North Shoal Light. Routine, in February, meant only that everyone had rehearsed the complications.

I had loaded the heaviest items aft and low, counting each strap twice. The propane cylinders were upright in their rack, the food bins lashed under the port bench, and the sensor case wrapped in a wool blanket even though the manufacturer claimed it was rated to minus forty. Marta, the station keeper, had written in the margin of the request form: PLEASE KEEP CONNECTOR DRY. She underlined dry twice. The last sensor had failed after saltwater found its way into a hairline crack near the cable gland.

At 06:15 the harbor lights were behind us and the channel markers had become silhouettes. The marine forecast called for northwest winds rising from twelve to twenty knots by noon, with snow squalls forming over open water. Our timing depended less on speed than on the landing. North Shoal has no proper pier. The station sits on a granite ledge with a short steel ladder and a narrow unloading shelf that is usable only when the swell is low enough. If the wind turned early, we could reach the island and still be unable to transfer a single box.

I checked the route against the paper chart even though the plotter was working perfectly. In winter I prefer redundant information. The electronic display tells me where we are; the chart reminds me what is around us. The distinction matters when blowing snow erases the horizon and every black patch of water looks like a channel. Our planned track kept us east of Gull Reef, then bent north across a deeper basin before approaching the light from the lee side.

By seven, the sky had gone from black to a flat blue-gray. Leon poured coffee from a thermos and held the cup against the wheelhouse heater before handing it over. We talked through the unload sequence again. Mail and electronics first, because they were light and vulnerable. Food next. Fuel-related supplies last, because a propane cylinder swinging on a line can turn an awkward landing into an emergency. The two of us could unload quickly, but only if nobody improvised.

The first complication came at 07:22. A band of snow moved across the basin from west to east, not heavy enough to stop us but dense enough to reduce visibility to a few hundred meters. I cut speed. The radar showed the island as a bright irregular return ahead, while the lighthouse itself disappeared completely. For twelve minutes we navigated by instruments and compass, listening to ice scrape along the hull. When the squall passed, the tower appeared suddenly, white against dark rock, close enough that we could see frost on the gallery rail.

Marta was waiting in a red survival suit. She raised one arm and pointed toward the lee pocket beside the unloading shelf. The water there was moving, but not violently. I made one slow approach, backed away, then tried again with less angle. Leon stepped forward only after I called that we were stable. He looped the bow line through the ring without tying it hard; in these conditions the boat needed room to rise.

The transfer became a practiced rhythm. Marta caught the first dry bag and slid it behind the windbreak. I passed up the weather sensor case, then the mail sack. Leon handled the food bins. One contained oranges, which had become a minor tradition after a keeper once wrote that winter stations received every imaginable canned food but almost no fresh fruit. The crate of lamp housings was heavier than expected, so we rigged the short lifting line rather than trying to muscle it onto the shelf.

The propane cylinders forced us to wait. A set of larger swells arrived from the northwest, lifting the stern and pushing the bow away from the rock. Nobody said anything for several minutes. There is a particular discipline in standing beside cargo while conditions are temporarily wrong: the job is not delayed by indecision; it is delayed because waiting is the correct action. When the swell eased, we transferred both cylinders separately, with Marta clear of the line of swing.

Inside the service room, the lighthouse felt warmer than it actually was. Condensation formed on my glasses. Marta showed us the failed weather sensor mounted outside the equipment cabinet and explained that its humidity readings had started jumping by twenty percentage points within seconds. We did not install the replacement; the keeper had a maintenance procedure for that, and our role was delivery. I did, however, compare the serial number on the new unit with the manifest before she signed for it.

We stayed twenty-eight minutes on the island. The forecast was already changing. On the handheld radio, the coast station announced that winds at the offshore buoy had reached eighteen knots, earlier than predicted. That shortened our margin. We took the outgoing mail, an empty gas cylinder rack insert, and a small box containing water samples for the mainland lab.

Departure required more care than arrival. I waited until the boat rose level with the shelf, Leon stepped aboard, and Marta released the looped line. We backed into open water before turning. The lighthouse receded quickly once we cleared the lee side, its tower sharper now under a strip of pale sunlight.

The return run was rougher but simpler. The important decisions had already been made: cargo delivered, no schedule pressure left, no reason to hold speed. We arrived at the harbor at 10:36, forty-four minutes earlier than the point at which the forecast expected the worst winds. I logged engine hours, fuel use, sea state, and the snow-squall delay. Then I noted one operational change for the next winter run: move the weather-sensitive electronics into a hard waterproof case rather than relying on wrapping alone.

The supply manifest ended with a line for remarks. I wrote, “All items delivered; transfer paused for swell; no damage or injury.” It looked almost too brief for the morning we had just had. But that is often what a successful logistical run becomes on paper: a sequence of small hazards converted, one by one, into uneventful facts.
