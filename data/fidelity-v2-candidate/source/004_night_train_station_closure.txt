At 11:17 p.m., the station clock stopped showing departures.

For most of the evening it had behaved as it had for decades, clicking from one line to the next while the last trains of the branch line moved through Bellweather Junction. Then the operator in the control room typed a final command, and the amber letters on the concourse board disappeared. No cancellation message replaced them. The screen became a black rectangle in a brass frame.

Mara noticed before anyone else. She had been sweeping rainwater from the tiles near the east doors, pushing a gray fan of water toward the drain. When the board went dark, she rested both hands on the broom and looked up as if the building itself had stopped speaking.

The station was closing at midnight. Not for repairs, not for a holiday schedule, but permanently. The railway had announced the decision eleven months earlier, after ridership fell and the new bypass line opened six kilometers south. There had been petitions, a council motion, two public meetings, and a proposal to turn the branch into a seasonal service. None changed the timetable. The 23:42 to Harbor City would be the final passenger train.

People had come to watch it leave.

They stood beneath the iron canopy in wet coats, some with tickets and some without. Teenagers photographed the old enamel signs. A retired conductor named Mr. Vale wore his uniform cap though he had not worked for the railway since 1998. Someone had placed flowers beside the stationmaster’s office, which made Mara think of a funeral and then feel annoyed at the comparison. Buildings did not die. They were emptied, altered, demolished, reused. She had spent twenty-three years cleaning this one and knew better than most how much life in a station consisted of things left behind.

At 11:24 a family arrived dragging two suitcases. They were not there for the ceremony. The father looked at the silent departure board, then at his phone, then asked Mara whether the Harbor City train was still running.

“Yes,” she said. “Platform two. Last one.”

He smiled with visible relief. His daughter, perhaps six years old, was carrying a pillow shaped like a fox. She did not know that she was about to board a historic train. She only wanted to know whether there would be a window seat.

That pleased Mara.

The station had accumulated too much meaning during its final week. Every bench had become “the old bench,” every light fixture “the original lamp,” every delay “one of the last delays.” Ordinary inconvenience was being polished into memory before it had even finished happening.

At 11:31 the café shutter came down for the final time. Tomas, who had leased the kiosk for twelve years, passed paper cups of coffee through the narrowing gap until there were none left. He had sold the refrigerator in the morning and unplugged the pastry case after lunch. A handwritten sign on the counter read NO CHANGE LEFT, EXACT COINS ONLY. Beneath it, someone had added THANK YOU in blue ink.

Mara walked the concourse collecting cups. The station smelled of wet wool, coffee, brake dust, and the faint mineral damp that rose from the basement whenever it rained. She knew which sounds would vanish first after closure. The rolling suitcase wheels. The pneumatic hiss of train doors. The announcements bouncing under the roof. Other sounds would remain: gutters, pigeons, the freight trains that would still use the through track at night.

At 11:36 the signal beyond the curve changed from red to green.

A murmur ran through the crowd. Phones lifted. Mr. Vale stepped closer to the yellow line, then checked himself and moved back. Mara found that small gesture more touching than the flowers.

The train arrived slowly, headlights diffused by rain. Its driver gave one short horn blast before entering the platform. People applauded, which made the passengers already aboard look confused. The doors opened. For several minutes the station resumed its practical purpose. Travelers stepped off. Travelers stepped on. A woman ran down the stairs because she was late. The conductor answered questions about connections. The family with the fox pillow found seats in the third carriage.

The ceremony had no official speech. The railway had offered to send a regional manager, but the community group declined after arguing over who should speak first. In the end, the platform had only the sounds of people talking among themselves.

Mara preferred that too.

She had spent the afternoon cleaning the stationmaster’s office for the inventory team. In the bottom drawer of a cabinet she found a packet of luggage labels printed with destinations that had disappeared from the railway map years ago: Mill Creek, Orison, West Point Ferry. She could have taken one. Nobody would have noticed. Instead she put the packet on the desk with the keys.

At 11:42 the conductor raised a lamp. The doors closed in sequence.

The crowd became quiet enough that Mara could hear rain striking the platform roof.

The train moved. Slowly at first, then with the familiar gathering pull that made the carriage windows slide past like frames of a film. People inside waved. People outside waved back. The last carriage carried a red marker light. It crossed the end of the platform, passed the signal, and shrank along the wet track until the curve took it.

For several seconds everyone continued looking at the empty rails.

Then the station began closing not as an idea but as a list of tasks.

Police officers guided visitors toward the main exit. Tomas carried two cardboard boxes to his van. The control operator printed a final log sheet. Mara locked the waiting room at 11:51 after checking beneath every bench. A scarf remained on one seat, dark green and still damp. She hung it on the lost-property hook even though the lost-property office would cease to exist in nine minutes.

At 11:56 Mr. Vale approached her. Without the train there, his old uniform cap seemed suddenly theatrical.

“Will they keep the clock?” he asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Should.”

He looked up at it. The hands still showed the correct time. Unlike the departure board, the clock had not been switched off.

At midnight, the station doors were supposed to be locked. Mara waited until the digital watch on her wrist changed to 00:00, then turned the key in the east entrance. There was no alarm, no final announcement. The automatic lights remained on because their timer did not know the station had closed.

She and the operator left through the staff door at 00:08.

Outside, rain had become mist. Cars were departing the lot. The tracks shone under orange lamps. Across the street, a new bus stop had been installed for the route that would begin service at five in the morning.

Mara stood beneath the awning while the operator searched for his keys.

“What happens tomorrow?” he asked.

She knew he meant the building, but she answered literally.

“Contract crew comes at nine. They start removing equipment.”

He nodded.

She did not tell him that she had been reassigned to the bypass station, a glass-and-concrete building with automatic floor scrubbers and no leaking east doors. She had visited it once. The platforms were wider. The elevators worked. The staff room had windows. It was, by every practical measure, a better station.

Before leaving, Mara looked through the locked doors. The concourse was empty, but not transformed. The benches were still there. The clock continued moving. The green scarf hung behind the glass on a hook labeled LOST PROPERTY.

That, she thought, was the difficulty with endings. They rarely arranged themselves to look finished.
