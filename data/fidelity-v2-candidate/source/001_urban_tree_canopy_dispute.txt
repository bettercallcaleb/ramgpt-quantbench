Municipal Hearing Digest: Urban Tree Canopy Dispute

The Planning and Environment Committee devoted nearly three hours of its Thursday session to a dispute over the city’s proposed tree-canopy bylaw, a policy that would require replacement planting or a fee when mature trees are removed during redevelopment. The hearing drew residents from older low-rise neighborhoods, apartment tenants, builders, arborists, and representatives of two business associations. Although speakers broadly agreed that summer heat and storm damage have made urban trees more valuable, they disagreed sharply over who should bear the cost of preserving canopy on private land.

Staff opened with a map showing that overall canopy coverage had remained close to 28 percent for a decade while its distribution became less even. Several central neighborhoods retained mature street trees and deep backyards, but industrial districts and newer subdivisions averaged less than half that amount. The city’s heat-vulnerability analysis also showed that blocks with the lowest canopy tended to record higher afternoon surface temperatures during July heat events. Staff emphasized that the proposed bylaw was not designed to freeze the existing landscape. Its stated purpose was to slow net canopy loss while allowing redevelopment to proceed.

The draft policy would classify privately owned trees by trunk diameter and condition. Removal of a large healthy tree would trigger either a replacement requirement based on canopy equivalency or a payment into a municipal planting fund. Dead, hazardous, invasive, or structurally unsound trees could be exempted after inspection. Small residential projects would receive a simplified permit process, while developments removing multiple significant trees would submit an arborist plan.

The first group of deputants argued that the proposal was too weak. A neighborhood association from Ward 6 presented photographs of three blocks where older houses had been replaced by larger buildings with underground garages. According to the association, thirty-seven mature trees had been removed over six years and only eleven replacements survived beyond their second summer. Its spokesperson said the city treated replacement as a matter of counting saplings rather than rebuilding canopy. She asked for survival bonds, larger minimum planting areas, and stronger penalties when approved protection fencing was ignored during construction.

An arborist who followed her made a narrower technical point. He cautioned against a simple “one tree removed, one tree planted” rule because a newly planted tree cannot immediately replace the shade, habitat, and stormwater interception of a mature specimen. He recommended using projected canopy area after twenty years as the accounting unit. He also warned that species choice mattered: a row of small ornamental trees might satisfy a numerical replacement target while providing little long-term shade.

Builders and property owners focused on cost and predictability. A representative of the Small Residential Builders Council supported permits for hazardous-tree exemptions but objected to the proposed fee schedule. He presented a hypothetical infill project where two large maples near the rear property line would conflict with excavation for a foundation. Under the draft formula, he said, the replacement obligation could exceed the landscaping budget for the entire site. He asked for a cap tied to project value and argued that residents would ultimately pay the fee through higher housing prices.

Several councillors challenged that example. One asked whether the conflict could be reduced through a smaller excavation footprint. Another noted that the city already permits design changes for setbacks, drainage, and utility conflicts, suggesting that tree retention should be considered at the same stage rather than after a building envelope has been finalized. The exchange exposed a recurring disagreement in the hearing: developers tended to describe tree removal as a consequence of an otherwise settled design, while preservation advocates treated site design itself as negotiable.

Apartment tenants raised a different concern. Speakers from two high-rise neighborhoods said the debate was overly focused on large trees in affluent detached-house areas. Their buildings had minimal shade at entrances, playgrounds, and transit stops. One tenant described waiting for a bus beside a six-lane road where the nearest mature tree was more than a hundred meters away. She supported the replacement fee only if revenue were directed toward low-canopy neighborhoods rather than automatically spent near the property where a tree was removed.

That proposal received support from the public health unit, whose representative cited the city’s heat-risk index. The unit recommended that at least half of fee-in-lieu revenue be assigned using heat vulnerability, pedestrian exposure, and existing canopy deficit. It also asked the city to coordinate planting with sidewalk reconstruction so newly planted trees would receive adequate soil volume rather than narrow pits surrounded by compacted fill.

The most contested testimony came from residents with aging trees close to homes. One homeowner described a large silver maple that had dropped branches during two storms. Her insurance company had advised her to address the risk, yet an earlier city inspection classified the tree as retainable. She worried that the proposed bylaw would force homeowners to choose between liability and a large removal fee. Staff responded that the draft contained a hazardous-tree exemption but acknowledged that inspection turnaround times would need improvement if permit volume increased.

Committee members also heard from the local utility. Its forestry manager said poorly coordinated planting can create long-term conflicts with overhead lines, underground services, and sightlines. He asked for a shared species and siting guide rather than separate municipal and utility standards. In response, staff agreed that some existing tree-planting rules were contradictory: one document encouraged broad-canopy species, while another required clearances that made those species difficult to place on narrow streets.

During questions, the committee requested three revisions before the bylaw returns for a vote. First, staff will model a graduated fee that reflects tree size but includes a ceiling for small residential projects. Second, the exemption process for hazardous trees will be rewritten with defined service timelines and an appeal mechanism. Third, the planting fund will include an equity formula so money collected in high-canopy districts can support priority planting in hotter neighborhoods.

The chair resisted calls to settle the matter immediately. She summarized the hearing as a dispute not over whether trees have public value, but over how that public value should be recognized when the trees themselves stand on private property. The draft bylaw assumes that canopy loss creates costs beyond the property line: hotter sidewalks, faster runoff, reduced habitat, and less shade. Opponents do not necessarily reject those effects; they question whether an individual permit is the right place to recover them.

The committee voted 6–2 to receive the testimony and direct staff to return with amendments within ninety days. No final tree-removal rules were adopted at the hearing. Until the revised draft is published, the central policy question remains unresolved: whether the city can design a system strong enough to protect mature canopy without turning every difficult tree on a private lot into a financial dispute.
