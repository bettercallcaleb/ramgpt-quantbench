A nonlinear equation

f(x)=0

can sometimes be rewritten as a fixed-point problem

x=g(x).

Starting from x_0, fixed-point iteration generates

x_{n+1}=g(x_n).

The algebraic equivalence f(x)=0 <-> x=g(x) is not enough to guarantee convergence. Different rearrangements of the same equation can have completely different numerical behavior.

Consider

x^3+x-1=0.

This equation has exactly one real root because

f'(x)=3x^2+1>0,

so f is strictly increasing, with f(0)=-1 and f(1)=1. The root alpha lies in (0,1), numerically near 0.6823.

One fixed-point rearrangement is

g_1(x)=1-x^3.

Then a fixed point satisfies x=1-x^3, or x^3+x-1=0.

The derivative is

g_1'(x)=-3x^2.

At the root,

|g_1'(alpha)|≈3(0.6823)^2≈1.397>1.

This predicts local divergence. If x_n is close to alpha and e_n=x_n-alpha, Taylor expansion gives

e_{n+1}
=g_1(alpha+e_n)-g_1(alpha)
=g_1'(alpha)e_n+O(e_n^2).

For small errors, magnitude is multiplied by approximately 1.397, so perturbations grow.

A second rearrangement is

g_2(x)=(1-x)^(1/3),

defined for x<=1. Its derivative is

g_2'(x)=-(1/3)(1-x)^(-2/3).

At the root, use 1-alpha=alpha^3:

g_2'(alpha)
=-(1/3)alpha^(-2).

With alpha≈0.6823,

|g_2'(alpha)|≈0.716.

This is less than one, so local linear convergence is expected.

A third rearrangement is

g_3(x)=1/(1+x^2),

because x^3+x=1 implies x(1+x^2)=1 for the positive root.

Then

g_3'(x)=-2x/(1+x^2)^2.

At alpha, since 1/(1+alpha^2)=alpha,

g_3'(alpha)=-2alpha^3.

Because alpha^3=1-alpha≈0.3177,

g_3'(alpha)≈-0.6354.

This contraction is stronger than g_2 near the root, so asymptotically it should converge faster.

A local derivative test is useful, but a global convergence theorem needs an interval mapped into itself. Suppose g maps [a,b] into [a,b] and

|g'(x)|<=L<1

for every x in [a,b]. Then g is a contraction. Banach's fixed-point theorem guarantees:
1. a unique fixed point alpha in [a,b];
2. convergence x_n->alpha for every x_0 in [a,b];
3. the a priori error bound

|x_n-alpha|
<= L^n |x_0-alpha|;

4. the computable a posteriori bound

|x_n-alpha|
<= L/(1-L) |x_n-x_{n-1}|.

For g_3 on [0.6,0.75],

g_3(0.6)=1/1.36≈0.7353,
g_3(0.75)=1/1.5625=0.64.

Because g_3 is decreasing, it maps the interval into approximately [0.64,0.7353], which is contained in [0.6,0.75].

Its derivative magnitude is

|g_3'(x)|=2x/(1+x^2)^2.

On this interval the maximum is below one. Evaluating at representative endpoints gives roughly 0.649 at 0.6 and 0.614 at 0.75; the maximum in the interval remains around the mid-0.65 range. Thus the contraction theorem applies.

Linear convergence means

lim_{n->infinity} |e_{n+1}|/|e_n| = lambda

with 0<lambda<1. For differentiable g with g'(alpha) nonzero,

lambda=|g'(alpha)|.

If g'(alpha)=0 but g''(alpha) !=0, Taylor expansion gives

e_{n+1}
=(g''(alpha)/2)e_n^2+O(e_n^3),

which is quadratic convergence.

This suggests modifying an iteration so its derivative vanishes at the root. Newton's method can be written as fixed-point iteration

g_N(x)=x-f(x)/f'(x).

At a simple root alpha,

g_N'(alpha)
=1-[f'(alpha)f'(alpha)-f(alpha)f''(alpha)]/[f'(alpha)]^2
=0.

This explains Newton's local quadratic convergence.

For f(x)=x^3+x-1,

x_{n+1}
=
x_n - (x_n^3+x_n-1)/(3x_n^2+1).

Starting at x_0=0.7,

f(0.7)=0.343+0.7-1=0.043,

f'(0.7)=2.47.

So

x_1≈0.7-0.043/2.47≈0.682591.

The next correction is already much smaller.

Yet Newton is not globally safe. If f'(x_n) is tiny, the step can be huge. For functions with multiple roots, iterates may enter another basin of attraction. A contraction mapping on a certified interval gives stronger global guarantees than a bare local Newton analysis.

Fixed-point acceleration can improve a linearly convergent sequence. Aitken's Delta-squared method uses three successive iterates:

x_hat_n
=
x_n - (x_{n+1}-x_n)^2 /
       (x_{n+2}-2x_{n+1}+x_n),

provided the denominator is nonzero.

If errors behave approximately geometrically,

x_n=alpha+c lambda^n,

Aitken's formula eliminates the leading geometric term exactly in the ideal model.

For vector equations x=g(x), the scalar derivative is replaced by the Jacobian J_g(alpha). Local convergence is governed by its spectral radius:

rho(J_g(alpha))<1

is the key asymptotic condition under appropriate smoothness. A matrix norm satisfying ||J_g||<1 on a region gives a contraction argument.

Stopping criteria also require care. A small step

||x_{n+1}-x_n||

does not automatically imply a small residual ||f(x_n)|| or small error ||x_n-alpha||. Under a contraction with known L,

||x_n-alpha||
<= L/(1-L)||x_n-x_{n-1}||,

so step size becomes meaningful. Without such a bound, it is prudent to monitor both step size and residual.

Divergence can be subtle. If g'(alpha)=-0.99, iterates may alternate around the fixed point and converge slowly. If g'(alpha)=-1.01, the first several iterations may look nearly stable before the error grows. If g'(alpha)=1, the first derivative test is inconclusive and higher-order terms decide behavior.

The design of a fixed-point solver therefore begins before iteration. One chooses a representation g, identifies a region expected to contain the solution, checks whether g maps that region into itself, estimates a contraction factor, and only then interprets observed convergence. The same nonlinear equation can generate a stable solver, a slow solver, or a divergent solver depending entirely on that choice.
