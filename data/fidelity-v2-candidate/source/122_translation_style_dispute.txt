Translation Style Review

Participants:
Wei — translator
Hannah — English-language editor
Sora — bilingual reviewer
Martin — project manager

Martin: We need to resolve the style disagreement before the remaining chapters are translated. The issue isn't whether the English is grammatical. It's how much texture from the Chinese source should remain.

Hannah: My concern is readability. Some passages currently feel translated in a way that draws attention to themselves.

Wei: And my concern is that the proposed edits erase the narrator's rhythm and social register.

Sora: Let's use specific examples instead of arguing about "literal" versus "natural" in the abstract.

Martin: Agreed.

Sora: First sentence in chapter three. Source: "她嘴上说不急，手里的茶盖却一下一下刮着杯沿。"

Wei's version: "She said she was in no hurry, but the lid in her hand scraped the rim of the teacup again and again."

Hannah's edit: "She said there was no rush, though she kept worrying the teacup lid against the rim."

Wei: "Worrying" is elegant, but it gives the motion an English literary flavor the narrator doesn't have.

Hannah: "Scraped the rim again and again" sounds mechanical.

Sora: What information matters? She verbally denies urgency, while repetitive hand movement betrays tension.

Wei: Yes, and "一下一下" is plain, repetitive language.

Hannah: What about "She said she wasn't in a hurry, but kept scraping the lid along the rim of her teacup."

Wei: Better. "Kept" carries repetition without ornament.

Sora: I agree.

Martin: Record the principle?

Sora: Preserve plain repetitive diction when the source is deliberately plain. Don't automatically replace repetition with a more literary English verb.

Hannah: Fine.

Sora: Second example: "人情不是秤砣，哪有一斤一两算得清。"

Wei: "Human obligations aren't weights on a scale; you can't calculate every pound and ounce."

Hannah: I changed it to "Relationships aren't an account book; you can't balance every debt."

Wei: But that's a different metaphor.

Hannah: The original weight units don't carry naturally.

Sora: The metaphor is culturally ordinary but understandable. We don't need to replace it just because English has another idiom.

Martin: Would "Favors between people aren't weights on a scale; you can't measure every ounce exactly" work?

Wei: "Favors" is too narrow for 人情 here.

Sora: Context includes favors, obligation, and social feeling. Maybe: "What people owe each other isn't a weight on a scale; you can't measure it down to the last ounce."

Hannah: That's natural enough.

Wei: I like that because it keeps the scale.

Martin: So metaphor preservation is default unless it becomes unintelligible.

Hannah: Default, not absolute.

Sora: Correct.

Sora: Third issue: forms of address. The source repeatedly calls an older neighbor "王姨."

Wei: I used "Auntie Wang."

Hannah: I changed it to "Mrs. Wang" because she isn't related.

Wei: That loses the social relationship.

Sora: "Auntie Wang" is understandable in contemporary English translations, especially with context.

Hannah: But some readers will assume actual kinship.

Martin: Can the first occurrence clarify?

Wei: "Auntie Wang, as everyone in the lane called her..." That's intrusive.

Sora: We can let context establish it. The narrator calls several unrelated older women Auntie plus surname.

Hannah: Then I can accept "Auntie Wang" if the style sheet defines kinship terms as social address when context supports them.

Martin: Good.

Sora: Fourth: idiom. Source: "他这人三棍子打不出一个屁来."

Hannah: The literal draft, "You couldn't beat a fart out of him with three sticks," is impossible in this scene.

Wei: I agree that literal wording is comic in English in the wrong way.

Sora: Function?

Wei: It means he's extremely taciturn, with a rough colloquial tone.

Hannah: I proposed, "You could pull his teeth before you'd get a sentence out of him."

Sora: That's vivid, but introduces a different image.

Wei: "You couldn't get three words out of him" is too neutral.

Martin: Could we use "You couldn't beat a word out of him"?

Hannah: That's idiomatic enough and retains roughness.

Wei: Yes, though it loses the exaggeration count.

Sora: Acceptable functional substitution. Note that idioms can be replaced when literal transfer produces a misleading tone.

Martin: Next.

Sora: Sentence-final particles and short dialogue. The grandmother says, "行了行了，别说了，吃饭吧。"

Wei: "All right, all right. Enough talking. Eat."

Hannah: "That's enough. Let's eat."

Wei: My version is intentionally abrupt.

Hannah: In English "Eat." sounds hostile.

Sora: Is she hostile?

Wei: Not exactly. She is ending an argument through authority.

Hannah: "All right, enough. Let's eat." retains control without sounding bizarre.

Wei: Yes. Repeating "all right" is less important than the turn-taking function.

Sora: Good.

Martin: So not every surface repetition must survive if English pragmatics change the character.

Wei: Correct, but we should know why we're removing it.

Sora: Fifth: paragraph structure. Hannah merged three one-sentence paragraphs.

Hannah: Because they looked choppy.

Wei: The source uses those breaks around a moment of realization.

Sora: Read them.

Wei: "The door was open. / Her shoes were gone. / Only then did he look at the clock."

Hannah: In my edit: "The door was open and her shoes were gone. Only then did he look at the clock."

Sora: The original pacing is stronger. The isolated facts arrive separately.

Hannah: I can restore the breaks.

Martin: Rule: preserve marked paragraph pacing unless English layout creates an actual comprehension problem.

Hannah: Yes.

Sora: Sixth: culturally specific object, "搪瓷缸."

Wei: "enamel mug."

Hannah: Fine.

Sora: No footnote?

Wei: No. The object is understandable.

Martin: Good. We don't annotate every ordinary object.

Sora: Seventh: "单位." In this chapter it's the father's work organization in the older state-enterprise context.

Hannah: "company"?

Wei: Too narrow.

Sora: "work unit" is historically specific and conventional.

Hannah: But general readers may not know it.

Martin: First occurrence can be "work unit," with context doing the rest.

Wei: Exactly.

Sora: Footnote only if the institutional meaning matters later.

Martin: Now the bigger question: do we domesticate units and money?

Wei: Keep yuan as yuan. For physical measures, context-dependent.

Hannah: I don't want jin translated as "catty."

Sora: If exact measure matters, "jin" can confuse readers. If approximate household quantity, translate the functional amount into kilograms only if the conversion doesn't erase historical voice.

Wei: In dialogue, a grandmother saying "buy two jin of pork" should not suddenly sound metric.

Hannah: Could say "two jin—about a kilogram" once?

Martin: That feels explanatory inside dialogue.

Sora: Better to use "two jin of pork" and put a concise glossary if the term recurs often.

Hannah: I can accept that.

Martin: We're converging on a style philosophy: fidelity isn't word-for-word sameness; it's preserving narrative function, register, metaphor, social relationships, and pacing unless doing so creates a false effect in English.

Wei: Yes.

Hannah: And readability isn't permission to normalize every unusual feature.

Sora: Exactly.

Martin: Let's capture operational rules:
1. Preserve metaphor by default.
2. Preserve social address where it carries relationship.
3. Replace idioms when literal transfer creates the wrong tone.
4. Preserve marked repetition and paragraph rhythm unless English pragmatics distort them.
5. Avoid unnecessary explanatory footnotes.
6. Keep culturally meaningful terms when context or glossary can carry them.
7. Every substantial stylistic change should answer: what source effect are we preserving?

Hannah: I would add: don't make the English more elegant than the source.

Wei: Absolutely.

Sora: And don't make it rougher just to signal foreignness.

Martin: Excellent. We'll apply this to chapter four as a pilot. Wei translates, Hannah edits with tracked rationale on disputed lines, Sora reviews only those flagged points. If the system works, we use it for the rest.

Hannah: That's much better than arguing globally over whether the translation should be "literal."

Wei: Agreed. The useful question is always: literal about what?
