My grandmother’s recipe notebook does not contain a recipe for rice pudding.

This would not matter except that everyone in the family remembers her making it. We remember the chipped blue pot, the skin that formed on top while it cooled, and the argument over whether raisins belonged in it. Yet the notebook, which otherwise records everything from dumpling fillings to pickled beans, is silent.

I inherited the notebook after my mother cleaned out a kitchen drawer and decided that paper stained with oil should not be stored beside tax receipts. It is a small black book with a cracked spine. The first pages are written in my grandmother’s tight handwriting. Later pages contain my mother’s rounder letters, then mine, then a few additions from cousins who were apparently incapable of writing without arrows and exclamation marks.

The notebook does not read like a cookbook. Measurements are inconsistent. “One cup” might mean an actual measuring cup or a particular ceramic mug that no longer exists. Oven temperatures appear only in the newer entries. Instructions assume knowledge: fry until ready, add enough flour, cook the usual way. Some ingredients are listed without quantities because, presumably, anyone sensible would know how much onion belongs in a pan of that size.

As a teenager I found this infuriating. I wanted recipes to behave like laboratory procedures. I asked my grandmother how many teaspoons of salt she used in potato pancakes.

She said, “Depends on the potatoes.”

“How much?”

“Enough.”

I interpreted this as evasiveness. Years later, after trying to make the same pancakes from potatoes with radically different moisture content, I understood that she had given me a technical answer.

The notebook records adaptation more clearly than tradition. One cabbage roll recipe has three different fillings written in different decades. The earliest uses pork and rice. A later note says “beef if Peter coming.” Another version reduces meat and adds mushrooms. My mother’s handwriting replaces a canned tomato product with passata. None of these changes is marked as a departure from authenticity. They are simply what happened next.

When people talk about family recipes, they often imagine a stable object passed intact through generations. Ours behaved more like a conversation. Each cook inherited a method, changed it under pressure from ingredients, health advice, migration, equipment, children’s preferences, and grocery-store availability, then wrote down only part of the change.

The most revealing page is headed CHRISTMAS COOKIES but contains four unrelated batches. My grandmother’s original dough calls for lard. My mother added “butter OK, chill longer.” A cousin wrote “orange zest—trust me” in green ink. I added a note about weighing flour because packing it into cups produced inconsistent results.

Nobody crossed out the older instructions. The page accumulated alternatives.

For several years I tried to clean the notebook up. I typed recipes into a spreadsheet with standardized units. I converted handfuls into grams, clarified ambiguous oven settings, and separated ingredients from instructions. The result was useful. It was also strangely lifeless.

In the spreadsheet, a soup recipe became a list of quantities followed by numbered steps. In the notebook, the same recipe includes my mother’s warning not to add noodles until the last fifteen minutes, a stain that smells faintly of celery if the page gets damp, and my grandmother’s note beside bay leaves: “remove before serving, unlike your uncle.” The joke depends on a story nobody fully remembers.

I stopped treating the notebook as raw data to be normalized.

That does not mean I stopped measuring. In fact, I became more precise in my own cooking. I weigh flour for baking. I record temperatures when they affect texture. I write timings that another person can reproduce. But I now keep those details beside the original phrasing instead of replacing it.

The notebook became especially important when my son started asking where particular dishes came from. His question was usually geographic: which country, which town, which side of the family? The answers were rarely neat.

One stew has a name associated with my grandmother’s childhood region, but the version we cook uses vegetables she could not easily buy there. A dumpling filling includes a spice my mother adopted after moving to a different city. A cake that everyone calls “Grandma’s cake” was apparently learned from a neighbor whose name nobody remembers.

The notebook shows that family food can be rooted without being pure. Recipes travel because people travel. They acquire substitutions, shortcuts, and borrowed techniques. Sometimes the borrowed version becomes the one that carries the strongest memory.

The missing rice pudding taught me this most clearly.

After my grandmother died, three relatives attempted to recreate it. My mother remembered that the rice was cooked partly on the stove before baking. My aunt insisted it went directly into the oven. One cousin was certain the raisins were soaked first. I remembered no raisins at all, probably because I picked them out.

We each made a batch.

None tasted exactly right.

At first this felt like failure. Then my aunt pointed out that we were comparing our puddings not with one known recipe but with decades of slightly different bowls served under different circumstances. The “original” we were chasing may never have existed in a single fixed form.

I wrote all three versions into the notebook.

There was barely enough space, so I used two pages and taped in an extra sheet. I titled the section RICE PUDDING — RECONSTRUCTIONS. Underneath I listed what each person remembered and where those memories disagreed. I did not choose a winner.

My son now makes the version with no raisins. He adds cardamom, which nobody in the previous generation used. The first time he did it, I had the impulse to say that Grandma never put cardamom in rice pudding. I stopped myself because Grandma also never wrote down the recipe.

Instead I asked him how much he used.

“A little,” he said.

“How much is a little?”

He shrugged. “Enough.”

There are moments when inheritance announces itself with almost embarrassing clarity.

The notebook’s physical condition is getting worse. The binding has loosened and several pages are brittle. I scanned the whole thing at high resolution and store copies in two places. The scans preserve information and may outlast the paper. Still, when I cook from it, I use the original.

That choice is partly sentimental and partly practical. The notebook lets me see sequence. I can tell which notes were added later, which ink faded, which recipe was used often enough to stain the page. A digital transcription tends to flatten those layers unless someone deliberately preserves them.

One day the original book may become too fragile for the kitchen. If that happens, the scans and standardized recipes will take over. I no longer think that means the tradition has failed. The tradition was never the object alone.

It was people standing at different stoves, looking at what they had, remembering what someone else once did, and deciding what “enough” meant this time.
