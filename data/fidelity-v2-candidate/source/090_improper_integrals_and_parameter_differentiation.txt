Parameter differentiation can turn an improper integral that is difficult to evaluate directly into a differential equation for a family of easier integrals. The method is powerful, but the interchange of differentiation and integration must be justified.

Consider, for a>0,

F(a)=integral_0^infinity [e^(-ax)-e^(-x)]/x dx.

Each term separately would produce a logarithmic divergence near x=0 if divided by x without cancellation, but their difference is well behaved. Expanding near zero,

e^(-ax)=1-ax+O(x^2),
e^(-x)=1-x+O(x^2),

so

[e^(-ax)-e^(-x)]/x
=(1-a)+O(x).

At infinity, both exponentials decay. Thus F(a) converges for every a>0.

Differentiate formally with respect to a:

F'(a)
=
integral_0^infinity [-x e^(-ax)]/x dx
=
-integral_0^infinity e^(-ax) dx
=
-1/a.

If this differentiation is valid, then

F(a)=-log a + C.

Since F(1)=0,

C=0,

and therefore

integral_0^infinity [e^(-ax)-e^(-x)]/x dx
=-log a.

To justify the derivative locally around a fixed a_0>0, restrict a to an interval [alpha,beta] with 0<alpha<a_0<beta. The derivative of the integrand is

-e^(-ax).

For every a in [alpha,beta],

|e^(-ax)| <= e^(-alpha x),

and e^(-alpha x) is integrable on [0,infinity). Dominated convergence therefore allows differentiation under the integral sign.

A second example produces a classical rational integral. Define

G(a)=integral_0^infinity e^(-ax)/(1+x^2) dx,

for a>=0.

At a=0,

G(0)=integral_0^infinity dx/(1+x^2)=pi/2.

Differentiating gives

G'(a)
=
-integral_0^infinity x e^(-ax)/(1+x^2) dx.

This is not obviously simpler. Differentiating again gives

G''(a)
=
integral_0^infinity x^2 e^(-ax)/(1+x^2) dx.

Use

x^2/(1+x^2)=1-1/(1+x^2).

Hence

G''(a)
=
integral_0^infinity e^(-ax) dx
-
integral_0^infinity e^(-ax)/(1+x^2) dx
=
1/a-G(a),

for a>0.

Thus G satisfies

G''(a)+G(a)=1/a.

The resulting differential equation still contains a singular forcing term, so this route is more structural than elementary. It illustrates that differentiation does not guarantee an immediate closed form; it transforms the analytic problem.

A cleaner parameter family is

H(a)=integral_0^infinity e^(-x) sin(ax)/x dx,
a>=0.

Near x=0,

sin(ax)/x -> a,

and the exponential controls infinity, so the integral converges.

Differentiate:

H'(a)
=
integral_0^infinity e^(-x) cos(ax) dx.

The Laplace transform gives

integral_0^infinity e^(-x) cos(ax) dx
=1/(1+a^2).

Therefore

H'(a)=1/(1+a^2).

Since H(0)=0,

H(a)=arctan a.

Thus

integral_0^infinity e^(-x) sin(ax)/x dx
=arctan a.

Letting a grow suggests the Dirichlet integral. Write

H(a)=integral_0^infinity e^(-x) sin(ax)/x dx.

Substitute t=ax:

H(a)=integral_0^infinity e^(-t/a) sin t/t dt.

As a->infinity, H(a)->pi/2. Formally e^(-t/a)->1, suggesting

integral_0^infinity sin t/t dt=pi/2.

But ordinary dominated convergence is not directly applicable on [0,infinity), because |sin t|/t is not integrable. A separate oscillatory convergence argument is required. This is a good example of why a correct parameter calculation does not automatically justify every limiting operation afterward.

Parameter differentiation also evaluates integrals involving logarithms. For s>-1 define

I(s)=integral_0^1 x^s dx.

Direct integration gives

I(s)=1/(s+1).

Differentiate with respect to s:

I'(s)
=
integral_0^1 x^s log x dx
=
-1/(s+1)^2.

At s=0,

integral_0^1 log x dx=-1.

Differentiate again:

I''(s)
=
integral_0^1 x^s (log x)^2 dx
=
2/(s+1)^3.

Thus

integral_0^1 (log x)^2 dx=2.

More generally,

integral_0^1 x^s (log x)^k dx
=
(-1)^k k!/(s+1)^(k+1).

This follows by differentiating k times.

The same idea underlies the gamma function. For s>0,

Gamma(s)=integral_0^infinity x^(s-1)e^(-x) dx.

Differentiation yields

Gamma'(s)
=
integral_0^infinity x^(s-1)e^(-x) log x dx,

and higher derivatives insert higher powers of log x.

Why is differentiation allowed? On a compact parameter interval s in [alpha,beta] with alpha>0, split the integral into (0,1] and [1,infinity). Near zero,

x^(s-1)|log x|^k
<= x^(alpha-1)|log x|^k,

which is integrable for alpha>0. At infinity,

x^(s-1)e^(-x)|log x|^k

is bounded by a polynomial-log factor times e^(-x), also integrable. This provides a dominating function.

Improper integrals often require two independent checks: convergence of the original integral and control of the differentiated integrand uniformly over a parameter neighborhood. It is not enough that both

integral f(x,a) dx

and

integral partial_a f(x,a) dx

happen to converge pointwise for each a. A theorem such as dominated convergence, uniform convergence, or a suitable Leibniz rule must support the interchange.

A practical workflow is therefore:

1. Define the parameter domain carefully.
2. Check all improper endpoints.
3. Compute the formal parameter derivative.
4. Find an integrable bound independent of the nearby parameter.
5. Differentiate under the integral.
6. Solve the resulting differential or algebraic relation.
7. Determine the integration constant from a convenient parameter value.
8. Justify any later limit separately.

The elegance of the method comes from choosing the family well. The integral of interest should appear at a convenient parameter value, while differentiation should remove the troublesome part of the integrand. The analysis that justifies the interchange is what converts that elegant heuristic into a valid proof.
